ISO 9001 Documented Information: the Complete Guide to Clause 7.5

What counts as documented information, how long to retain it, and the review workflow auditors actually want to see.

JG
Juliana Geremias Published Sep 2, 2026 · 9 min read

If you've sat through an ISO 9001 certification audit, you already know the moment: the auditor asks for a record, and what you hand over says more about your QMS than any policy document ever could. That's the real function of Clause 7.5 — Documented Information, and it's worth understanding properly instead of treating it as paperwork.

What counts as documented information

ISO 9001:2015 replaced the old split between "documents" and "records" with a single term: documented information. It covers anything your organization must control and maintain — plus the medium it lives on, whether that's a PDF, a spreadsheet, a signed paper form, or an entry in your QMS software.

The standard requires documented information in two situations:

  • Where ISO 9001 explicitly requires it (the quality policy, objectives, scope, and a handful of specific records).
  • Where your organization determines it's necessary for the QMS to work — which is most of it, in practice.

Quick definition

Documented information is information your organization must control and maintain, plus the medium it's held on — a QMS record, a policy PDF, or a signed paper checklist all qualify equally.

How long you actually need to keep it

The standard doesn't set a universal retention period — that's a common misreading. What it requires is that you define a retention period appropriate to each type of record, and follow it consistently. For most quality records, auditors expect to see retention tied to your certification cycle: commonly 3 years, sometimes longer for records tied to product liability or regulatory claims.

"The retention period isn't a compliance afterthought — it's the difference between an audit finding and an audit non-conformity." — Juliana Geremias, Quality Systems Lead

The review workflow auditors want to see

Beyond the retention period itself, Clause 7.5.3 asks for evidence of control: information needs to be available where it's used, protected from unintended changes, and reviewed on a defined cycle. In practice, that means your auditor will look for:

  1. A clear owner for each controlled document, not just a folder it lives in.
  2. A visible revision history — version number, date, and what changed.
  3. An approval step before a new revision goes live.
  4. A way to prevent someone from working off an outdated version by mistake.

Common mistakes that trigger findings

Most nonconformities under this clause don't come from missing documents — they come from documents nobody controls. The usual suspects: a procedure that was updated informally but never re-approved, a record kept in two places with two different versions, and retention periods that exist on paper but aren't actually enforced anywhere. Fix the workflow, and the paperwork takes care of itself.
